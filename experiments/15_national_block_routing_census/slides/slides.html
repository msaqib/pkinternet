PTCL, Nayatel, Cybernet, Nova and Z-Com are vantage points. They are never destinations. The targets are always the 747 blocks of the 48 small ISPs.

Why several vantage points? Because a detour depends on the sender's transit — the same block can be local from one network and foreign from another.

Method

The sampling rule

  • The unit is the BGP prefix, not the address. Every address in an announced block shares one route — so the block is what we census
  • Every block is kept. None ranked, pruned or sampled away — a census claim needs completeness. Reduction happens inside blocks, never across them
  • We probe K addresses inside each block — the next three slides are what K is
  • Density comes free — how many of the K replied, from the same pass

Prefix-as-unit and density come from TASS (Klick et al., IMC 2016). We invert their rule: TASS drops low-density prefixes; a census must keep every one.

K · 1 of 3

K is how many addresses we probe per block

So we probe K evenly spaced addresses instead and let the BGP route do the generalising: every address in the block shares one route, so a handful should tell us how the whole block is reached.

“Should” is the word doing the work. The next slide tests it.

K · 3 of 3

How often does the assumption break?

Measured on the June run — 1,758 block-and-vantage pairs that got the full K=8:

OutcomeShareMeaning
All probed addresses agree79.4%the block has one route — small K is safe
Addresses disagree20.0%the block is genuinely split
No usable answer0.6%all eight silent or inconclusive
  • One block in five is split. “The route to a block” is not always a well-defined thing — which is itself a finding, and one nobody has published
  • On the blocks that are unambiguous, a random K=2 reproduces the K=8 verdict 98.6% of the time
  • So K=2 gets the verdict right and the picture wrong. It cannot see that a block is split, because with two samples there is nothing to compare
  • Decision: keep K=8. A 20% split rate is too high to sample away. Reduce vantages or rounds if the run needs shrinking — never K

Classification · 1 of 2

What counts as a detour

What we call tromboning is what the measurement literature calls a routing detour — a path between two endpoints in one country that leaves and returns.

  • Edmundson et al., 2016 — Characterizing and Avoiding Routing Detours Through Surveillance States. Establishes the detour as a systematic property of interdomain routing, and how to identify it from traceroute paths
  • Gupta et al., PAM 2014 — Peering at the Internet's Frontier: ISP Interconnectivity in Africa. The direct regional antecedent: African traffic detouring via Europe because local interconnection was missing
  • This census asks that same question of Pakistan

How we decide it left the country

Geolocation is used — but never on its own. Every foreign-hop claim is gated by RTT.

RuleValueWhy
Foreign hop must also be slow≥ 40 msRouter geolocation databases disagree (Gharaibeh, IMC 2017), so a country code alone is not evidence. RTT bounds how far a hop can be (Gueye et al., 2006)
Inter-hop jump≥ 60 msPK→Singapore measures ~60–90 ms — no domestic leg does that
Any hop RTT≥ 70 msClear of the domestic ceiling; PK→Europe is ~100–130 ms
Stays local< 45 msDomestic ceiling plus queueing headroom
Known artifactAS6327Shaw CPE hop on the Nova probe — physically in PK at ~1.5 ms
  • Thresholds are ours, calibrated to observed Pakistani RTTs. The principles are from the literature above
  • The RTT backstop matters: 1,140 of the corrected run's 1,562 detours rest on it alone, where no foreign hop ever replied

Reading the result

One number, hiding two different things

The corrected June run comes out at 8.6% of probes detouring. That single figure is an average over two axes that behave completely differently:

  • Who you send to — the destination ISP whose block is being probed. Ranges 0.6% to 64.5% across the 48 ISPs
  • Where you send from — the vantage point the traceroute starts at. Ranges 4.0% to 46.3% across the 7 probes

Two spreads of roughly the same size means the detour is not simply a property of the badly-connected ISP. The next three slides work through what it actually is.

Reading the result

Why the per-ISP average misleads

Pace Telecom reads 27.4% overall (194 of 708). That average is built from vantages ranging from 80.0% to 6.1%, and it survives the squatted-address correction intact.

  • PTCL Karachi 80.0% (72 of 90) against Nayatel Islamabad 6.1% (7 of 115), on the same 17 blocks
  • No network experiences 27.4%. It is the mean of a broken path and six better ones, and it describes nobody
  • This is why the deliverable is a matrix, not a league table of ISPs
  • Caveat, stated on the slide: 59 of PTCL's 72 verdicts here rest on the RTT backstop with no foreign hop resolved. The spread is real; the mechanism is not yet pinned down

It also shows why balanced coverage matters so much. If PTCL happened to contribute more probes than the others, Brain's average would climb — without anything in the network having changed.

Payoff

What we might find

  • A per-ISP detour rate and its real spread — quotable, because balanced
  • Whether the vantage or the destination dominates. If it is the pair, the fix is bilateral peering; if the destination, that ISP's transit contract
  • Who the detour transits through — PTCL or Transworld, per ISP
  • Which foreign exchange it surfaces at — Equinix Singapore, DE-CIX, Omantel
  • How often a single block splits across two routes — the June data says one in five, and nobody has published that for anywhere
  • How sparse small-ISP address space really is
  • Whether any trace touches PKIX or PIE — the panel found 0 of 222,944
  • Announced-but-dark blocks, and the 18 licensed ISPs announcing nothing
27 / 28

Honesty

Limits we state up front

  • Density is an active-address proxy, not traffic — Atlas cannot see bytes. Report “% of active address space”, never “% of traffic”
  • A single round is a snapshot — rates are quoted as a range across rounds
  • Geolocation is used, but never alone. The residual risk is a hop that is both mis-geolocated and genuinely slow
  • Transit clustering is an assumption, only partly tested
  • The FLL roster includes a few large and LDI members — flagged, not dropped
  • Block sizes are not uniform — 22 of 747 are larger than /24, so the eight positions are computed per prefix length
  • The RTT rule can fire on last-hop rate limiting — a router answering slowly looks like distance. Traces whose only evidence is a spike on a repeated final hop need separating from real detours
  • Every run ships a readable routes file next to the CSV. No verdict without the paths to check it against
